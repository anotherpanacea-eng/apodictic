## 4. Evidenced strengths

These are judged relative to the writer's stated aim and the manifesto genre.

- **Clarity of ask.** The council is told exactly what to vote on and when [I1].
- **Concreteness.** Specific timing ("next week," "Monday," "immediately") makes the demand actionable [I1–I2].
- **A single memorable figure.** "40%" gives the argument one quotable anchor [I1].
- **Genre fit.** Brevity, imperative mood, and certainty match the self-declared manifesto form.
- **Internal consistency of direction.** Every directive points the same way; nothing hedges or contradicts the main ask.

## 5. Evidenced weaknesses

Each is tagged with its basis.

1. **Load-bearing claim unsupported.** The entire justification rests on "will reduce deaths by 40%," with no source, referent, or baseline [I1].
   *Basis:* the writer's aim. A consequential decision rests on this one claim. It is also a text-grounded reader promise, since "will" promises a definite outcome.
2. **Referent ambiguity in the 40%.** A reader cannot tell what the number measures [I1].
   *Basis:* text-grounded reader promise. A quantified claim implies a measurable quantity.
3. **Sequencing tension.** [I2]'s "Monday" and "immediately" may precede or bypass the vote requested in [I1].
   *Basis:* the writer's aim. If the steps appear to pre-empt the council's decision, that may cut against securing it. This is internal coherence; the actual dates are uncertain.
4. **Pre-emptive dismissal of comparison.** "There is no need to compare" asserts sufficiency without showing it [I2].
   *Basis:* the writer's aim. The audience is a deliberative body being asked to decide, and the text names comparison as a question while declining it.
5. **Maximal scope unexplained.** "Entire" and "all" raise the stakes, and the text gives no reason why partial measures are excluded [I1–I2].
   *Basis:* text-grounded. The scope words are the writer's own and create the question.
6. **Displaced users unaddressed.** "Cancel all clinic appointments immediately" [I2] creates an obvious within-text question: what happens to those patients? The passage is silent.
   *Basis:* text-grounded. The consequence follows from the passage's own directive.
7. **Program X undefined.** The beneficiary of the entire budget is a label only [I1].
   *Basis:* the writer's aim. The council is asked to fund something it is not told about.

## 7. Counter-readings

- **Genre reading.** Manifestos conventionally assert rather than argue. The evidence may exist elsewhere in the fiction, and the passage may be a rallying text, not the full case.
- **Urgency reading.** If the 40% claim is true within the fiction, the haste in [I2] could be read as moral urgency: delay costs lives. That would make "no need to compare" a rhetorical stance against paralysis rather than an evasion.
- **Conditional reading.** "Begin on Monday" and "cancel immediately" could be implicitly conditional on the vote passing. The text permits this reading but doesn't secure it.
- **Characterization reading.** In fiction, an overconfident manifesto can be a deliberate portrait of its speaker. However, the writer's confirmed aim of *securing* the decision weighs against reading it as intended irony.

- the council's rules, authority, and what it needs to be persuaded;

**Permitted behavior**
- Editing for clarity, force, structure, and internal coherence.
- Helping the writer strengthen the rhetoric of their position within the fiction.
- Flagging gaps, ambiguities, and sequencing issues, as above.
- Proposing repair *classes* for the writer to fill.

**Must be retained**

- Inventing opponents, alternatives, or institutional facts not supplied.
- Presenting the empirical claim as true or false.
- Predicting how the council or any audience would respond.

That scenario is not confirmed, so it is noted only as a boundary and not applied.
