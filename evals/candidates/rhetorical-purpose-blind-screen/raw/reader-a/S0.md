## 3. Commitment inventory

**Factual / presuppositional**
- A gathering is taking place "in this room tonight." (W1)
- The addressees live "here," since "however long you have lived here" presupposes residency. (W1)
- Being "pushed out" is a live possibility for members of the group. This is presupposed, not asserted as occurring now. (W2)
- The person beside each listener is part of "the family." (W2)

**Causal**

**Quantified**

**Representative**
- Speaks for and to a collective "us" of neighbors. (W1, W2)

**Prescriptive**
- Implicitly: treat everyone present as belonging. (W1, a declarative with normative force)

- Explicitly: "let them know they are not alone." (W2)

No policy, funding, numerical or attributed-source commitments are present.

## 4. Evidenced strengths

- **Aim alignment.** W1 explicitly removes tenure as a basis for belonging ("however long you have lived here"). That directly serves a resident-solidarity aim. *Basis: writer-stated aim.*
- **Principle-to-action movement.** W2 moves from a general maxim to a concrete, immediate, low-cost act toward a physically present person. That matches "asks residents to stand with one another." *Basis: writer-stated aim.*
- **Scope discipline.** The passage stays out of policy, consistent with the writer's statement. *Basis: writer-stated aim.*
- **Economy.** Two short spans carry welcome, rationale and directive. That suits an opening. *Basis: text-grounded. This describes structure, not a claim about audience effect.*

## 5. Evidenced weaknesses

- **Universal address vs. narrower presuppositions.** "Whoever you are" (W1) promises unconditional inclusion. "The family beside you" (W2) presupposes family units. Listeners who are single, in roommate households, or seated beside non-family may not map onto the directive. *Basis: text-grounded reader promise set by W1.* It may be idiomatic (see §7).
- **Residency presupposition.** "However long you have lived here" presupposes everyone lives here. This is low-severity given the writer's statement that the passage addresses residents. It becomes relevant only if non-residents attend, which is uncertain. *Basis: text-grounded.*
- **Unanchored referent.** "Pushed out" (W2) carries the passage's motivating weight, but its referent is not stated within the spans. Whether this is a weakness depends on context not supplied, such as whether the gathering is already known to concern displacement. *Basis: text-grounded.*
- **Bounded belonging.** "Belong in this room tonight" (W1) can be read as limiting belonging to the event. That sits in mild tension with a solidarity aim that seems to extend beyond the evening. *Basis: text-grounded, and an alternative reading exists.*

## 7. Counter-readings

- **"Family" as metaphor.** "The family beside you" may mean neighbors-as-family, not literal households. Read that way, the tension with W1 largely dissolves.
- **"Pushed out" scope.** It could mean eviction or displacement, social exclusion within the community, or exclusion from civic spaces like this one. Each reading shifts what "stand with" asks.
- **"Diminished."** It could be moral (the community is lessened), practical (lost networks or shared resources), or obligation-framing (pressure to act). The text does not select among these.
- **"Tonight."** It could emphasize presence and immediacy rather than limit belonging.
- **Current-event implication.** If displacement is happening to someone present, W2 could read as pointed. If not, it reads as general. Which applies, and how either lands, is unknown.
