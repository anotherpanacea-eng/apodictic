STORY

The shed roof had survived the storm, though the path to it had become a shallow channel. Bea waded through carrying the bakery's last thermos. On the far bank, her aunt was waiting on a porch step with a blanket around her knees.

Three launches stood side by side inside: two with red maintenance tags, one with a green tag. Every launch in the shed had spent the whole night and morning without an engine; the removed engines were still in the locked machine shop. No engine had been installed or taken out during Bea's visit.

Bea had worked here long enough to know the tags mattered more than the fresh paint. She went first to the hooks for a life jacket. The orange one smelled of wet rope. She had always worn the blue one as a child, but the orange straps would close over her aunt's winter coat.

The green-tagged launch belonged to the bakery. Its engine had never been removed since delivery, and the wire service seal across the mounts was unbroken. Bea checked the fuel and turned the starter. The engine caught, coughed, and settled. She ran it for a minute while clearing reeds from the slip.

At the porch she cut the engine and let the current bring her alongside. Her aunt refused to leave without the little tin in which she kept seed packets. Bea put it under the dry jacket before helping her aboard.

They returned with the engine running slowly, keeping the wash away from the flooded gardens. At the shed her aunt took a sip from the thermos and made a face. Bea tried it too. Neither of them had remembered to ask the baker whether he had filled it with coffee or hot water.

They sat on the green launch's bench until the shivering stopped. Her aunt opened the seed tin and began sorting the packets that would have to be dried first.
