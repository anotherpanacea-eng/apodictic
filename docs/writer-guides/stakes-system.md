# Stakes

> Escalation without payment is suspense vocabulary without suspense architecture.

Telling the reader that everything is at stake costs you nothing. Stakes become real only when the story makes your character pay, and keeps raising the price. The test is simple to state: at each major beat, can a reader say what your character concretely loses, in order, with the cost going up? If they can't, your draft has the tone of high stakes without the structure.

## What it looks like when it works

- A reader can name the consequence at every major turn.
- Kinds of stakes stack up (a life, a marriage, a reputation) rather than taking turns.
- Your character's position visibly tightens. Options close; they don't multiply.
- Cost is shown on the page, not just announced.
- The climax carries the most weight the story has built, not the midpoint.

## Common ways it goes wrong

- **Told, not felt.** Characters say the stakes are high, but no scene takes anything from them.
- **The plateau.** Pressure peaks in the middle and holds flat to the end.
- **The rescue.** Your character is cornered, then luck or a new ally lowers the pressure while the language stays urgent.
- **The disappearing threat.** The danger is set up, then the antagonist or deadline drops out of sight.
- **Cost amnesia.** A loss lands in one scene and is forgotten by the next.
- **The walk-away problem.** Nothing stops your character from leaving, so the reader wonders why they stay.

## When it's a choice, not a fault

Small stakes are fine if they escalate on their own scale: a quiet book can tighten just as surely as a thriller. Recurring stakes work if each return deepens what we understand. Stakes revealed late work if a careful reader could have assembled them from earlier clues. An anti-climax works if it still answers the book's central question. The fault is stakes that never move, not stakes that are modest.

## One question for your draft

List your major beats in order. Beside each, write what your character stands to lose right then. Does the list get worse as it goes, and does the worst entry sit at the climax?
