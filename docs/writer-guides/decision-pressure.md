# Decision pressure

> A choice without visible pressure is a twist without setup.

Your characters don't have to choose well. They have to choose in a way the reader can follow. When a character makes a big choice, readers ask at once: "Why didn't they just...?" The choice holds up if the page has already answered that question, either by weighing the other path or by making it plainly impossible. If it hasn't, the reader stops believing the choice, however dramatic it is.

## What it looks like when it works

- A reader can see the other options, or can tell why they're closed.
- Several pressures meet at the big choices: danger, a relationship, who the character is, what they don't know, time running out.
- The choice fits what the character knows at that moment, not what the reader knows.
- Fear, anger or exhaustion shapes how they decide, not just how they feel.
- Every choice costs something, and earlier choices box in later ones.
- Options narrow as the story goes, so the climax arrives as one hard choice.

## Common ways it goes wrong

- **The missing third option.** The scene offers two paths while an obvious third sits there, never ruled out.
- **"I have no choice."** A character says it, but nothing on the page has actually closed the doors.
- **Convenient stupidity.** A capable character turns careless just when the plot needs a mistake, then is sharp again.
- **Calm under fire.** The reverse: a terrified character plans as coolly as if nothing were wrong.
- **The free win.** A major choice pays off with no loss, no closed door, no bill that ever comes due.
- **The unprepared turn.** A big change of direction, at the midpoint or the climax, with nothing earlier pushing that way.

## When it's a choice, not a fault

A bad decision is fine if it is this character's bad decision, coming from their fears, values or blind spots. Frightened people make poor choices; that can be good psychology. You can hide a character's reasons on purpose, if a careful reader could rebuild them once the story is over. Spare, outward-facing prose can work without interior access, so long as the pressure is visible in action and dialogue. And a hard moral dilemma is not a gap if the reader can state both sides. The fault is a choice nobody could reconstruct from the page.

## One question for your draft

Pick your three biggest decisions. For each, write the option a doubtful reader would suggest instead. Does the page either weigh that option or make it impossible before the character chooses?
