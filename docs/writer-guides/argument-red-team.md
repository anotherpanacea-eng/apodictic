# The hostile reader

> The Red Team's value is not inventing new problems but showing how known structural features become exploitable under hostile conditions.

Someone who disagrees with you will read your piece: an opposition columnist, a committee member, a rival analyst, a peer reviewer. They won't invent new flaws. They will find the weak spots already in your draft and press on them: the shared premise under three claims, the alternative you never mention, the term that means two things. The test is whether your argument still stands when a smart, unfriendly reader uses your own words against you.

## What it looks like when it works

- You can name the strongest hostile question without panicking.
- You know where your narrowest defensible point is.
- Your key terms keep one meaning under pressure.
- Your concessions change the shape of the case; they aren't decoration.
- You can lose a side point without losing the whole argument.

## Common ways it goes wrong

- **The quotable sentence.** One line, quoted alone, makes you sound careless or extreme, and an opponent will find it.
- **The missing alternative.** You argue for your fix but never compare it with doing nothing or with a rival fix. A policy opponent will do that comparison for you.
- **The drifting term.** "Reform" or "equity" means different things in different paragraphs because nobody ever made you choose.
- **Overreach.** You claim more than your evidence or your standing supports. A witness who speaks past what they saw is easy to cross-examine.
- **Contradicting yourself.** You say one thing early and another later. A careful opponent will set the two side by side.
- **The buried answer.** The reply to an objection is already in your draft, pages away from the claim it protects. Move it; you don't need new material.

## When it's a choice, not a fault

Bold claims can be deliberate. If you open with "the system is broken," you're choosing to take on that burden. That's fine if the draft shows you know it: scope limits elsewhere, admitted tradeoffs, framing that's knowingly bold. Form matters too. An op-ed doesn't owe scholarly hedging in 900 words, a brief can lean on fuller support elsewhere, and plainness in an open letter isn't shallowness. Some exposure only matters for an audience you aren't writing for. The fault is risk you didn't see, or risk you saw but underrated.

## One question for your draft

Imagine your sharpest opponent has your piece open. Which single sentence would they quote, and which claim would they press first? Can you answer them from what's already on the page?
