# Compression

> The draft carries its construction equipment into the finished building.

A lot of what sits in your draft was there for you, not the reader. You needed to find your way in and work out what each scene was for. The reader doesn't need to watch that. The test for any passage: if you took it out, would anything later break or lose impact? If nothing breaks, it was scaffolding. If something breaks, keep the job it does, but ask whether it could do that job in half the words.

## What it looks like when it works

- A detail appears once, does its work, and the story moves on.
- Scenes start where the conflict starts and end once the outcome is clear.
- Transitions are cuts, not bridges.
- Emotional beats escalate instead of repeating.
- One concrete image does the work of a paragraph of explanation.

## Common ways it goes wrong

- **Telling it three times.** You state a point, show it, then remind us. The first time works. By the third, it drags.
- **Explaining the scene you just wrote.** A character spells out what the action or dialogue already showed.
- **The slow start.** The scene opens with arrival, settling in and small talk before anything happens.
- **The same feeling again.** Your character goes back over the same doubt at the same depth, and nothing moves.
- **The scene with no job.** You could remove it and nothing later would change. Sometimes it's a subplot that mattered in an earlier draft.
- **The guided tour.** Setting and world detail that shows off the place but puts no pressure on the story.

## When it's a choice, not a fault

Some material feels slow but is holding something up. A setup scene may carry information your climax depends on. A quiet processing beat may earn a later payoff. If it's the only place the reader learns something, or a reader would get lost later without it, keep it and tighten it instead. Genre moves the line too. Romance readers expect to sit with feelings, as long as the feelings change. Horror's slowness can build dread. A mystery's detour may be a planted trap. But meaning to write it isn't enough. A passage can be deliberate and still be one you can cut. When you're not sure, tighten before you delete.

## One question for your draft

Pick a scene that feels long. Mark where the conflict actually begins and where the outcome is clear. What sits outside those marks, and does anything later in the book need it?
