# Persuading a real audience

> Most persuasion failures are not "bad rhetoric" in isolation. They are mismatches between argumentative structure and the uptake conditions of the intended audience.

A sound case can still fail to land. The same claim can win one reader and lose another, depending on what you put first and what you leave unsaid. Persuasion is not polish. It is the order and framing that let a particular reader take the argument in. The test: can you name who you are writing for, and does each section arrive when that reader is ready for it?

## What it looks like when it works

- Your reader can tell why the argument matters to them.
- The order of points heads off the objections you can predict, rather than provoking them.
- Your concessions make you more trustworthy, not weaker.
- The support this reader finds most convincing comes first, not buried.
- Even cut to length, the chain from problem to recommendation stays intact.

## Common ways it goes wrong

- **Writing for everyone.** The draft addresses no reader in particular, so it serves none well.
- **Assumed common ground.** Background, definitions, or the link between evidence and claim are left out for readers who don't actually share them.
- **Asking before earning.** You ask for agreement, or for action, before showing why this reader should trust you or walking them through the problem and the tradeoffs.
- **The wrong values.** The piece is framed around what moves you, not what moves the reader you need.
- **The cheap concession.** A concession shows up too early, too late, or costs you nothing, so it does no work.
- **Preaching to the choir.** You lean on presumed agreement and stop arguing.

## When it's a choice, not a fault

Form changes the rules. An academic article may earn a late thesis by laying out literature and method first. Testimony is meant to sound formal. A policy brief compresses by design, so long as the logic survives the cuts. An op-ed may lack room for a full concession if the concession's work still gets done. An open letter can address audiences in layers, and advocacy can aim to rally supporters rather than convert opponents. The fault is a mismatch you didn't intend. And adapting should clarify, not just intensify; it should never hide what a reader needs to judge your case.

## One question for your draft

Name the one reader whose agreement you most need. Read your draft as them and mark where they first stop trusting or following you. What did they need to see before that point?
