# Claim, warrant, objection

> A text can have accurate data and a reasonable claim, and still fail structurally because the principle connecting them is absent, contested, or relies on an audience assumption the text never earns.

Good evidence and a sensible conclusion are not enough. Between them sits a bridge: the reason this evidence proves this claim. Leave it unstated and your argument works only for readers who already agree. The test: point to your evidence and ask "why does this prove my claim?" If the answer needs a principle you never wrote down, a skeptical reader won't supply it.

## What it looks like when it works

- A reader can state your claim in one sentence, and it holds from start to finish.
- The link between evidence and claim is on the page, or obvious to your actual readers.
- Your confidence matches your evidence. "In this case" stays "in this case."
- Concessions change something. The claim narrows, or a qualifier moves.
- Key terms mean the same thing in the conclusion as in the opening.

## Common ways it goes wrong

- **The missing bridge.** The evidence is real and relevant, but the reason it proves the claim lives only in your head.
- **The shifting word.** A term like "safety" or "harm" quietly changes meaning between premise and conclusion.
- **One story, whole system.** A true, vivid anecdote is asked to prove how things always work.
- **Hedges that evaporate.** "May" and "in some cases" appear early, then vanish, and the conclusion sounds surer than anything before it.
- **The free concession.** You acknowledge the objection, then carry on as if you hadn't. Delete the passage and nothing changes.
- **The uncompared proposal.** You beat the status quo but never name the next-best alternative.

## When it's a choice, not a fault

Your audience sets the bar. A sympathetic audience can share warrants you need not spell out; an organizing memo or fundraising appeal may skip objections altogether. Experts share compressed reasoning a general reader would need explained. An op-ed can leave out implementation detail a policy brief must carry. Some arguments work through story or many voices rather than thesis and evidence. That is fine if a reader can still find the claim and weigh the evidence. The fault is a form that hides weakness, not a form that is unconventional.

## One question for your draft

Name your audience. Then write your main claim, your strongest evidence, and the one-sentence reason the evidence proves the claim. Would that audience accept the reason without being told, or is it missing from your page?
